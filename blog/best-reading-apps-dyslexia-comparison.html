Best Reading Apps for Dyslexia: Free and Paid Options

October 30, 2026 8 min read

There is no single best reading app for dyslexia. The right one depends on what your child reads, which devices you have, and whether you want to pay. Some of the most useful tools are already free on your phone or computer.

This guide compares free built-in tools, text-to-speech apps, and audiobook libraries. Every feature listed comes from the product's own website, help pages, or App Store listing, checked on October 30, 2026. Prices and features change, so check the official page before you pay.

What to look for

Most reading tools for dyslexia are built around text-to-speech: the device reads the words aloud, often highlighting each word so your child can follow along (Understood). Our article on whether text-to-speech helps with dyslexia looks at the research. When you compare tools, ask:

  • Can it read what my child reads? Web pages, PDFs, school documents, or paper worksheets. Reading paper needs a camera scanner (sometimes called OCR) (Understood).
  • Does it highlight words as it reads? This lets your child see and hear the text together.
  • Can you change the look of the text? Font, size, spacing, and colors. Our guide to the best font for dyslexia explains what helps.
  • Which devices does it run on, and does it need the internet?
  • What does it really cost after any free trial?

Free tools you may already have

iPhone and iPad: Speak Screen and Accessibility Reader

Apple devices can read text aloud even if VoiceOver is off. Speak Screen reads the whole screen when you swipe down with two fingers from the top, and Speak Selection reads only the text you select, with a highlight style you can change (Apple Support; iPad). Accessibility Reader shows the text of any app in a full-screen view with the fonts, spacing, and background colors you choose, and can read it aloud (Apple Support; iPad).

Good to know: it is free and built in. Our step-by-step guide shows how to set up an iPhone or iPad for a child with dyslexia.

Microsoft Immersive Reader

Microsoft describes Immersive Reader as a free tool, built into products including Microsoft Edge, Word, OneNote, and Teams (Microsoft). Microsoft says it was originally designed for readers with dyslexia and dysgraphia. In Edge it reads web pages aloud, can add space between lines, and has a line focus that shows one, three, or five lines at a time (Microsoft). In Word it can split words into syllables and color nouns, verbs, and adjectives (Microsoft).

Good to know: if the Immersive Reader button does not appear on a web page in Edge, you can select the text, right-click, and choose Open in Immersive Reader (Microsoft).

Google Chrome reading mode

Chrome's reading mode shows a page with fewer distractions and lets you pick the font, size, and color theme. On a computer it can also read the page aloud, highlight words or sentences as it reads, and change line and letter spacing (Google).

Good to know: read aloud is for Chrome on a computer, and reading mode does not work on every website (Google).

Reading apps

Voice Dream

Voice Dream reads PDFs, EPUB books, Word documents, PowerPoint files, and web pages aloud, and its voices work offline across iPhone, iPad, and Mac (Voice Dream). It highlights each word as it is spoken and has a built-in scanner for paper pages (App Store).

Cost: free to download, but a subscription is needed after a free trial (App Store).

Speechify

Speechify reads documents, articles, and PDFs aloud with word-by-word highlighting, and you can photograph a printed page to hear it (App Store). It has apps for iPhone, iPad, Android, Mac, and Windows, plus a web app and browser extensions (Speechify).

Cost and limits: the free plan has a limited set of voices and speeds; scanning and the natural voices are part of Premium, a paid subscription (Speechify).

Read&Write by Everway

Read&Write reads text aloud while highlighting words, offers a picture dictionary, and can tint the screen or add a reading spotlight. It works on Windows, Mac, Chromebook, iPad, iPhone, and Android (Everway).

Cost: it is a paid subscription, mostly sold to schools, with plans for individuals too (Everway). Ask your child's school whether it already has a license.

DyslexiaBuddy (our app)

Our app is a reading app for iPhone, iPad, and Mac. You can scan a page or open a PDF, then listen with natural voices while each word is highlighted. It adds a reading ruler, Bionic Reading, Focus Mode (one word at a time), dyslexia-friendly fonts and colors, and 14 free classic children's books. Premium adds Olly, a reading tutor that explains a page and breaks hard words into sounds.

Cost: free to download. Premium is $3.99 a month, $39.99 a year, or $69.99 once, with a 7-day free trial. Find it on the App Store.

Audiobook libraries for students

Learning Ally

Learning Ally is a nonprofit with more than 80,000 human-narrated audiobooks and textbooks for people with dyslexia, other learning differences, and blindness or low vision. Its app has synchronized highlighting and adjustable speed, and works on iPhone, iPad, Android, Chromebook, and the web (Learning Ally, Learning Ally).

Cost and eligibility: a family membership is a yearly fee. A professional confirms eligibility, but your child does not need an IEP or 504 plan. Your school may already offer it (Learning Ally, Learning Ally).

Bookshare

Bookshare is an ebook library with over a million titles. US students from pre-K through college can join at no cost if they have a qualifying print disability, and dyslexia is on the list. Its free reader app reads books aloud with karaoke-style highlighting (Bookshare, Bookshare, Bookshare).

Good to know: a professional, such as a teacher or doctor, confirms eligibility, and an IEP or 504 plan is not required (Bookshare).

How to choose

  1. Start free. Try the tools already on your devices for a week. They may be all your child needs for web pages and school documents.
  2. Ask the school. Schools often have licenses for Read&Write or Learning Ally, and can help confirm eligibility for Bookshare.
  3. Match the main job. For paper worksheets, choose an app with a camera scanner. For school books, look at an audiobook library.
  4. Use the free trial with your child. Let them pick the voice and the look of the text. A tool they like is a tool they will use.

Reading tools help your child get to the ideas on the page, but they work best alongside good reading teaching (Understood). If you are not sure whether your child's struggles are dyslexia, see our guide to the signs of dyslexia by age.
