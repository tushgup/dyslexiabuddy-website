Signs of Dyslexia by Age: What Parents Can Look For

October 21, 2026 7 min read

If reading seems harder for your child than for their friends, it is natural to wonder whether it could be dyslexia. The signs change as children grow, so what you notice in a four-year-old looks very different from what you notice in a teenager.

This guide walks through common signs at each age, what is a normal part of learning, and what to do next if you are worried. A list like this cannot tell you whether your child has dyslexia. Only a proper assessment can do that. But it can help you know what to watch for, and what to share with your child's teacher.

First, what dyslexia is

Dyslexia mainly affects reading, writing, and spelling. It can also affect things like remembering information, planning, and using numbers. It does not affect intelligence (NHS).

Dyslexia is a challenge with language, not only with reading. That is why some of the earliest signs show up in how a child speaks, rhymes, or remembers sounds, long before they are asked to read (Understood).

No child shows every sign. People with reading difficulties often have different mixes of symptoms, such as trouble sounding out words, slow reading, or poor spelling (NICHD). One sign on its own is rarely a reason to worry. A pattern that lasts is worth a closer look.

Preschool (about ages 3 to 5)

Before children read, dyslexia can show up in speech and play with sounds. Signs can include:

  • Trouble learning nursery rhymes, or not noticing that words like cat, bat, and rat rhyme (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity).
  • Trouble learning the names of letters, even the letters in their own name (Yale).
  • Mixing up the sounds in words, like saying beddy tear for teddy bear (Understood).
  • Trouble finding the name of a familiar object, and saying thing or stuff instead (Understood).
  • Trouble with sequences, like the alphabet song, or with directions that have several steps (Understood).
  • Learning to speak later than other children (NHS).

Family history matters too. Dyslexia often runs in families, so reading or spelling difficulties in a parent or sibling are worth keeping in mind (Yale).

Kindergarten to second grade (about ages 5 to 7)

This is when reading lessons begin, and when many parents first notice that something feels harder than it should. Signs can include:

  • Trouble linking letters to their sounds, such as the letter b with the “b” sound (Yale).
  • Not being able to sound out simple words like cat or map (Yale).
  • Guessing from pictures or meaning instead of the letters, like reading puppy when the word is dog, or house when the word is home (Yale, Understood).
  • Trouble splitting a word into its sounds, or blending sounds back into a word (Understood).
  • Saying reading is hard, or quietly disappearing when it is time to read (Yale).

What about writing letters backwards?

Mixing up b and d is very common in young children and is usually not a sign of dyslexia on its own. Most kids grow out of it by the end of second grade. If your child still reverses letters a lot after that, it is a good idea to talk with their teacher (Understood).

Third to fifth grade (about ages 8 to 10)

By now, school expects children to read to learn. Signs often look like:

  • Slow, effortful reading (Yale), and trouble sounding out new words (Understood).
  • Skipping or mixing up small words like for and of when reading aloud (Understood).
  • Trouble recognizing common words quickly, even words they have seen many times (Understood).
  • Spelling the same word right and wrong in the same piece of work (Understood).
  • Messy written work with lots of spelling mistakes, or trouble telling the time and remembering the order of things like the days of the week (NHS).
  • Avoiding reading, especially reading out loud, or getting upset when they have to read (Understood, Yale).

Feelings are part of the picture. Children with dyslexia can become frustrated, and this can show up as acting out or as pulling away at school and at home (NHS).

Tweens and teens

Older kids often find clever ways around reading, so the signs can be easier to miss. Look for:

  • Reading that is still slow and takes a lot of effort, and homework that takes a very long time (Yale, Understood).
  • Searching for words, using vague words, or mixing up words that sound alike, like tornado and volcano (Yale).
  • Trouble with jokes, idioms, and puns (Understood).
  • Understanding a page much better when someone reads it to them (Understood).
  • Running out of time on tests, or finding a foreign language very hard (Yale).
  • Low self-esteem that is not always easy to see (Yale).

Look for strengths, too

A list of signs can feel heavy. It helps to remember that it describes only one part of a child. Many children with dyslexia are curious and imaginative, are good at seeing the big picture, and understand stories very well when they are read to them (Yale). Struggling with reading does not mean a child is not smart (Understood).

What to do if you see signs

  1. Write down what you notice. Note which words or tasks are hard, and when. Patterns are easier to see on paper, and your notes help others help your child (Understood).
  2. Talk to the teacher. Share what you see at home and ask what they see in class (Understood).
  3. Ask about an evaluation. In the US, you can ask your child's school for a free evaluation (Understood). In the UK, start with the school's special educational needs coordinator (SENCO). A GP cannot diagnose dyslexia, but can check things like hearing, and may suggest an eye test. A full dyslexia assessment is usually done by an educational psychologist or a specialist teacher (NHS).
  4. Tell your child there is help. Let them know you are working with their teacher, and that reading skills can improve with the right teaching (Understood, Understood).
  5. Keep reading fun at home. Read to your child, share audiobooks, and praise effort. Our parent's guide to dyslexia support at home and 10 reading strategies have ideas you can start today.
