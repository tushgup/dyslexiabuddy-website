Text-to-Speech for Dyslexia: Does Listening While Reading Help?

October 28, 2026 6 min read

When every word takes effort, a page of homework can feel like a wall. Text-to-speech lets your child hear the words while they see them. Many parents wonder whether that really helps, or whether it is a shortcut that gets in the way of learning to read.

Here is what text-to-speech is, what the research says so far, and how to use it so it supports reading instead of replacing it.

What text-to-speech is

Text-to-speech, often called TTS or read-aloud technology, reads digital text out loud. Many tools highlight each word as it is read, so your child can see the text and hear it at the same time. The reading speed can usually be made faster or slower (Understood).

It is built into most phones, tablets, and computers, and it works on documents and web pages. Some tools can also read the words in a photo, which is useful for paper worksheets (Understood). An audiobook is a little different: it is usually a recording of a person reading a whole book (Understood).

What the research says

The research is encouraging, with some honest limits.

  • Across many studies, it helps understanding a bit. A 2018 meta-analysis pooled studies of text-to-speech and similar read-aloud tools for students with reading difficulties. On average, these tools gave a modest boost to reading comprehension. The authors also said more research is needed to learn when and for whom it works best (Wood and colleagues, 2018).
  • Children with dyslexia may benefit the most. A 2023 study tested children aged 8 to 12 with reading and language difficulties. They understood passages better with text-to-speech than when they read silently. Children who had dyslexia only (without a wider language difficulty) got the biggest benefit (Keelor and colleagues, 2023).
  • Highlighting is still an open question. In that same study, text-to-speech with word highlighting and without it gave similar results. The researchers said more study of features like highlighting is needed (Keelor and colleagues, 2023).

In short: text-to-speech is not magic, but it is a helpful support for understanding what is on the page.

Is listening a shortcut?

This is the worry many parents have. The good news is that listening does not slow down the growth of reading skills. Understood explains that audiobooks and text-to-speech will not hold kids back, and many experts say listening can help kids become better readers (Understood, Understood).

Listening is also not “cheating”. The point of reading is to take in information, and how that information gets to the brain does not matter (Understood).

One important point

Text-to-speech supports reading, but it does not teach it. Tools like this on their own will probably not improve reading skills, so a child who struggles still needs the right reading teaching (Understood). Think of text-to-speech as a ramp that gets your child to the ideas, while reading lessons build the stairs.

Why it can help

  • It frees up energy for meaning. When sounding out words is hard, a child can spend all their effort on decoding. Hearing the words lets them focus on what the text means (Understood).
  • It uses eyes and ears together. Seeing a word while hearing it makes reading multisensory, which can help children connect letters to sounds (Understood).
  • It keeps kids in the conversation. Listening lets a child enjoy the same books as their classmates, which can make them more motivated and less frustrated (Understood).
  • It builds independence. Reading tools can help people feel more confident and do more on their own (Understood).

How to use it well

These are practical ideas to try at home. Every child is different, so let your child help decide what works.

  1. Follow along with the eyes. Encourage your child to look at the words while they listen, not to look away. Word highlighting makes this easier to do.
  2. Find the right speed. Start a little slower than normal speech, then speed up as your child gets comfortable.
  3. Pick a voice they like. A voice that sounds pleasant makes listening feel less like a chore.
  4. Use it for the hard parts. Instructions, word problems, and long chapters are good places to start. Keep some time for reading practice without it, too.
  5. Turn paper into audio. For printed worksheets, use a tool that can scan a page and read it aloud.
