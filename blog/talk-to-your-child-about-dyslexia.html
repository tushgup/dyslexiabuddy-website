How to Talk to Your Child About Their Dyslexia

November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Maybe you just got a diagnosis. Maybe your child has started asking why reading is so hard for them and so easy for their friends. Either way, you want to find the right words. You do not need a perfect speech. What helps most is honesty, warmth, and many small conversations over time.

Here is why the conversation matters, what to say at different ages, and how to help your child feel capable and speak up for what they need.

Why it helps to talk about it

Many kids notice that they read and write differently from other kids, even before they hear the word dyslexia (Understood). Struggling with any skill can make kids feel like they are not smart (Understood). Without an explanation, a child may fill in the gap with their own story about why, and it is rarely a kind one.

A name can change that. Children are often relieved to learn that there is a name for why reading is so hard (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity). It can also be a relief to know that there is a reason for their challenges, and that there are things that can help (Understood).

Four messages to get across

You will explain dyslexia in your own way, but these four ideas are worth coming back to.

  1. It has nothing to do with being smart. Dyslexia does not affect intelligence (NHS). It is also not about being lazy (Understood).
  2. Their brain works differently with words. Understood suggests explaining dyslexia as a difference in how the brain works that is not related to how smart someone is (Understood).
  3. They are not the only one. About ten percent of people are believed to be dyslexic (British Dyslexia Association). There are probably other kids at their school who are dyslexic too.
  4. Things can get easier. Dyslexia is lifelong, but with the right support, dyslexic people can become highly successful students and adults (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity). Special teaching and supports at school, like extra time on tests, can help people succeed in school and at work (Understood).

Words you can use, by age

These are only examples. Change them so they sound like you, and follow your child's questions.

Younger children

Keep it short and concrete. One idea from Understood is to tell your child that they are just as smart as other kids, and their brain just works a little differently (Understood). You might add: "That is why reading feels tricky right now. It is not your fault, and we are going to find ways that help."

Older children

Older kids can handle more detail. You can use the word dyslexia and explain that it is a name for why some kids and adults find it hard to learn to read, write, and spell (Understood). You might say: "Your brain has a harder time matching letters to sounds. It has nothing to do with how smart you are. Lots of people have it, and there are ways to make reading easier."

Teens

Teens are often self-conscious and may not want to call attention to themselves (Understood). Ask what they already know, and what they want to know. Talk about what helps them, such as extra time, listening to text, or using their phone to read aloud. How you say it matters

  • Stay calm and hopeful. A parent quoted in Yale's guide says that how you talk about it is as important as what you say (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity). Your child will take their cue from your tone.
  • Listen to how they feel. Children with dyslexia may become frustrated (NHS), and constant struggles can lead to anxiety and lower self-esteem. Showing empathy goes a long way to helping kids cope (Understood).
  • Name their strengths. Yale's guide encourages parents to let a child's strengths, and not their weaknesses, define them (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity). Celebrate small wins and praise their effort (Understood).

Keep the conversation going

This is not one big talk. A parent in Yale's guide describes it as an ongoing dialogue, not a single conversation (Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity). Short check-ins work well: after a hard homework night, after a good day, or when a new question comes up in the car.

It also helps your child to know that the adults around them understand. Kids need to know that the school understands their challenges and has ways to help (Understood). If your child has extra needs at school, the school can work with you on a plan (NHS).

Help your child speak up

Over time, the goal is for your child to be able to say what they need. That skill is called self-advocacy: the ability to communicate what your needs are (Understood). Joining a school meeting about their support can be a next step in learning to speak up (Understood).

Go at your child's pace, though. It is important not to push kids to speak up if they are not ready (Understood). Practicing at home first, with one simple sentence like "Can I have the test read aloud?", can make it feel less scary.
