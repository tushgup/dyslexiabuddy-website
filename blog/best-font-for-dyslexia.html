The Best Font for Dyslexia: What the Research Actually Says

October 20, 2026 6 min read

Search for "dyslexia font" and you will find special fonts that promise easier reading. The honest answer is simpler: no single font works for everyone. Spacing, size, and color matter at least as much, and what the reader likes matters most.

Here is what the research says, which settings tend to help, and how to try them in a few minutes on an iPhone or iPad.

1. Do special dyslexia fonts work?

Fonts like OpenDyslexic and Dyslexie were designed for readers with dyslexia. Their letters have heavier bottoms and more distinct shapes, so letters like b and d are harder to mix up.

The research has not found that they make people read faster or more accurately. In a 2017 study in Annals of Dyslexia, students in grades 3 to 6 with dyslexia read no faster and no more accurately in OpenDyslexic than in Arial or Times New Roman. Dyslexia Scotland's 2025 guidance says the same: these fonts are not necessarily better than standard fonts, so choose by preference.

That last part matters. Many readers say a dyslexia font feels calmer or friendlier, and feeling comfortable makes it easier to keep going. If your child likes OpenDyslexic, there is no reason to take it away. Just don't expect the font alone to fix reading.

2. Spacing helps more than the font

When letters sit close together, they crowd each other, and that makes words harder to pick apart. A 2012 study in PNAS tested this with 94 children with dyslexia in Italy and France, ages 8 to 14. With extra-large spacing between letters, they read more than 20% faster and made far fewer errors, with no extra training.

Current guidance builds on this:

  • Letter and word spacing: a little wider than normal. Too wide can slow reading again, so adjust until it feels right.
  • Line spacing: about 1.5 times the normal height, so the eye can find the next line.
  • Short lines: long lines make it easy to lose your place.

3. Fonts that tend to work well

Plain, familiar fonts with clear, evenly spaced letters are a good start. Many readers prefer sans serif fonts:

  • Arial, Verdana, and Calibri: simple and available almost everywhere.
  • Atkinson Hyperlegible: made by the Braille Institute so that similar letters, like l, I, and 1, are easy to tell apart.
  • OpenDyslexic: worth trying if your child likes how it looks.

Avoid italics, decorative fonts, and fully justified text, which leaves uneven gaps between words.

4. Size, color, and layout

  • Size: at least 12 point on paper, and bigger on screens. Let the reader make it larger.
  • Background: an off-white, cream, or peach background with dark text is often easier on the eyes than bright white.
  • Layout: left-aligned text, short paragraphs, and clear headings.

5. Find what works for your reader

The best test takes five minutes. Pick one short paragraph. Show it in two or three different setups: another font, more spacing, a warm background. Have your child read each one, then ask which felt easiest. Keep that one.
