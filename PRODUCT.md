# Product

## Register

brand

## Users

DyslexiaBuddy serves children, teens, adults with dyslexia, and the parents, educators, and support professionals who help them read with less friction. Visitors arrive with practical reading problems: homework pages, worksheets, textbooks, work documents, confusing passages, and moments where text feels tiring or inaccessible.

The website must speak to both the reader and the buyer/supporter. It should feel calm enough for dyslexic readers, credible enough for parents and educators, and specific enough that the app's assistive technology does not sound like generic AI.

## Product Purpose

DyslexiaBuddy is an iPhone and iPad reading coach for dyslexia. It turns physical or digital text into a more readable, listenable, and explainable experience: camera scanning, dyslexia-friendly reading settings, sentence and word tracking, tap-to-start audio, natural on-device voices, and a document-grounded tutor that can define, simplify, quiz, summarize, and answer comprehension questions.

The website's job is to convert App Store visitors by showing the real product experience, the accessibility value, the privacy posture, and the latest app capabilities without overpromising or making the reader work hard.

## Brand Personality

Calm, capable, and encouraging.

The brand should feel like a patient reading companion with real technical depth behind it. It can be warm and optimistic, but never childish or condescending. It should sound plain-spoken, specific, and grounded in reading outcomes: confidence, comprehension, privacy, and independence.

## Anti-references

Avoid generic AI startup tropes: purple gradients, abstract orbs, vague "unlock potential" copy, fake dashboard metrics, and glassy SaaS panels that do not show the app.

Avoid overstimulating accessibility design: harsh contrast for decoration, excessive motion, dense copy blocks, tiny labels repeated above every section, and visual flourishes that make scanning harder.

Avoid clinical or remedial framing. The site should not make dyslexia feel like a defect or homework punishment. It should avoid patronizing language, mascot-heavy childishness, and shame-based urgency.

## Design Principles

Show the reader, not just the promise. Use real app screenshots, reading surfaces, and concrete workflows whenever possible.

Practice what the product teaches. Keep typography legible, copy plain, line lengths controlled, motion optional, and contrast strong.

Make privacy and on-device intelligence feel reassuring. The tutor and voices should read as helpful, personal, and private, not invasive or magical.

Let warmth carry confidence. Use the app's orange, cream, and soft reading colors for approachability, balanced by dark ink, clean hierarchy, and credible proof.

Write for families and readers in the moment. Prefer concrete verbs like scan, listen, define, simplify, quiz, and resume over abstract claims.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Accessibility is a primary product promise. The website should target WCAG AA contrast, avoid text overflow, support reduced motion, and maintain readable defaults on mobile and desktop.

Use dyslexia-friendly typography where it helps reading surfaces, especially Atkinson Hyperlegible and app-relevant fonts like OpenDyslexic. Avoid long dense paragraphs, cramped line heights, tiny all-caps labels as a repeated scaffold, and interactions that require precision without clear affordances.

The privacy stance matters for children and families. Language should remain COPPA-aware, respectful of minors, and careful around camera, microphone, speech, and AI claims.
